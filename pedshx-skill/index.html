The paediatric history

A paediatric history is the usual history with the child's whole story added: the pregnancy and birth, feeding and growth, development and vaccines, then home and school. A teenager is also asked on their own. Each part is below in the order you do it, with what to ask and what to listen for. No course notes cover this yet; built from the Clinical Skills objectives and student notes. Anything beyond the objectives is tagged extra, and where an objective has no source behind it, the page says so.

General approach

Every paediatric history

The general approach

  • Say it first (TOSCE, Teaching Objective Structured Clinical Exam): "Hello, my name is [name], I'm a medical student working with Dr. [X]. I'd like to hear about [child's name] and what's been going on. Is it okay if I ask you both some questions, and I'll be taking a few notes?"
  • Family-centred approach: ask the child as well as the caregiver, and take the history of presenting illness (HPI) from the parent and from the child if they are able
    • use developmentally appropriate language: words that suit the child's age and stage
    • adapt to the family's needs and stay non-judgmental
  • Change your technique with the child's age: the course asks you to appreciate this for children and adolescents of different ages, but no source says how for each age, except that a teenager is asked partly on their own (section 8)
  • Special situations: an upset parent or an upset child: the objective asks you to deal with it, and no source describes how. Use the empathy steps in the Patient-Centred Interview summary note (name the feeling, listen first, explain plainly)
  • Family, community and society shape a child's health: ask about home, school and the child's world (section 7)
  • What a paediatric history adds (from the Paediatrics objectives): neonatal history, developmental history, growth and growth curves, nutrition, the immunization schedule, anticipatory guidance
  • After the encounter: evaluate peers' encounters and give constructive feedback, and reflect on your own, using the group's feedback to plan improvements

Pregnancy, birth, the newborn weeks

Antenatal (pregnancy)

How the pregnancy went

Ask
  1. "How was your pregnancy?"
  2. Any illness, medicines, smoking, alcohol or drugs?
  3. Any high blood pressure or diabetes of pregnancy?
Listen for

How many weeks (gestation) · infections, substances, high blood pressure, gestational diabetes

Birth

How the delivery went

Ask
  1. "Tell me about the birth."
  2. Early, on time or late?
  3. How was the baby born, and how much did they weigh?
Listen for

Spontaneous or induced · vaginal, forceps or C-section · birth weight

Post-natal (newborn period)

The first weeks

Ask
  1. "Did the baby need any extra care after birth?"
  2. Any yellow skin (jaundice), breathing problems or seizures?
  3. Did the baby come home with you?
Listen for

Jaundice, breathing problems, seizures · a stay in the newborn intensive care unit

Feeding and growing

Feeding

What and how much the child takes

Ask
  1. "How is the baby (or child) feeding?"
  2. Breast or formula, and for how long?
  3. When did solid foods start? How many wet diapers a day?
Listen for

Breast or formula, feeding problems, when solids began · wet diapers (a sign of enough fluid)

Growth

Whether they are growing as expected

Ask
  1. "Has [child] been growing the way you expected?"
  2. Has anyone said they are small or large for their age?
  3. Ask to see the child's health record for past weights and heights
Listen for

Slowed or stalled growth · past measurements to compare over time

Keeping pace

Developmental milestones

Whether the child is keeping pace

Ask
  1. "Is [child] doing what you'd expect for their age?"
  2. Ask in turn about big movements, hand skills, talking and understanding
  3. "How do they compare with other children, or with their brothers and sisters?"
Listen for

Behind peers in any one area · when they first crawled, walked and talked

The adolescent history (HEADSSS)

CS2 · Pediatrics Focused History · 8 areas to ask about

Start here

What to say

  • A thorough adolescent history has its own parts, and it is patient-centred, confidential and non-judgmental
  • See the teenager alone for part of the visit, after you have spoken with them and the parent together. Say what stays private and what does not before they tell you anything: you must pass on a risk of serious harm to them or to someone else
  • Student notes give the HEADSSS assessment: Home, Education or employment, Activities, Drugs, Sex, Suicide (mood), Safety
Tap to reveal

Setting it up

Time alone and confidentiality

A private, honest history

Ask
  1. Ask the parent to step out for part of the visit
  2. Say: "What you tell me stays between us, unless I'm worried about your safety or someone else's."
  3. Begin with easy topics
Listen for

Willingness to talk · what they want kept private

Closing the visit

CS2 · Pediatrics Focused History

Start here

How to finish

  • Summarize back to the child and the caregiver so they can correct you, then ask both what they were hoping for: FIFE (Feelings, Ideas, Function, Expectations) works for the child and for the parent
  • Thank them both and say what happens next
  • Give and receive feedback: evaluate a peer's encounter with specific, constructive comments, and reflect on your own, using the group's feedback to plan improvements
  • Anticipatory guidance (advice to a family about what to expect and how to keep the child safe) is named in the objectives, but no source lists what to cover. The only safety items covered are seatbelts and helmets for teenagers.

The ward visit: examining a child

CS2 · Pediatric Ward Visits

Start here

What the objectives say

  • Describe the physical examination of a child, putting the child's comfort first and staying flexible and opportunistic: do each part of the exam when the child lets you, rather than in one fixed order
  • Speak to the child in developmentally appropriate language, not only to the parent
  • Summarize and report both normal and abnormal findings
  • The course also says the physical exam should change with the child's age and stage
  • Not covered: no source describes the steps of a child's examination. This section keeps to what the objectives say, so check the method against your session.
