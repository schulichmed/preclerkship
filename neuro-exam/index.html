General approach

Every neuro exam

The general approach

  • Say it first (TOSCE, Teaching Objective Structured Clinical Exam): "Hi, I'm [name], a medical student. I'd like to examine your nervous system: I'll test your eyes and face, the strength, feeling and reflexes in your arms and legs, your coordination and your walking. Is that all right? Tell me if anything is uncomfortable."
  • Prepare: introduce yourself, check the patient's identity (wristband and chart), get consent, privacy, clean your hands before and after contact, warm hands and instruments, personal protective equipment (PPE) as needed
  • Equipment: pocket vision card (near card with pupil gauge), penlight, ophthalmoscope, cotton wisp, tuning forks (128 Hz for vibration, 512 Hz for hearing), reflex hammer, a single-use pin (safety pin or broken tongue depressor) and a dull object
  • Pin down the patient's words before you examine: "dizzy" and "black out" each mean several different things, so ask what they felt
  • Observe before you test, then explain each step in plain words and demonstrate on an area you are not testing
  • Order (the handbook's screening exam): orientation → language → cranial nerves → motor (observation, tone, pronator drift, power, reflexes, plantars) → sensory (pinprick, vibration) → coordination → balance (Romberg) → gait (walk, heels and toes, tandem, squat)
  • Positions: sitting for the head, the arms, arm tone and the arm reflexes · lying for leg tone and heel-shin · standing for Romberg and gait, last
  • Compare sides, and proximal (near the trunk) with distal (far)

Mental status and language

PPES 2 · Weeks 8–9 · 1 manoeuvres

What to look for

Before the manoeuvres

  • Appearance and conduct: dress, grooming, hygiene, posture, behaviour, any involuntary movements or obvious paralysis
  • While interviewing, screen orientation, memory and judgment
    • level of consciousness: alert, drowsy, somnolent, stuporous or comatose
    • orientation: oriented to person, place and time? able to obey commands?
    • attention: can they focus, answer questions and follow directions?
    • mood and affect: voice, facial expression, demeanour; ask how their mood has been
    • speech and language: clarity, amount, rate, fluency, comprehension, reading and writing
    • thought content: perceptions, judgment, memory
  • Folstein Mini-Mental State Exam (MMSE): perform, score and interpret later: PoM 2 Neurology: not covered by the sources used here; use the session's own form
  • Types of dysphasia (aphasia): aphasia is a problem with language, not with moving the mouth (that is dysarthria); language lives in the dominant hemisphere (usually the left)
    • expressive (Broca's): inferior frontal lobe · slow, halting, effortful speech, few words or none · understanding intact · usually aware of the problem
    • receptive (Wernicke's): posterior temporal lobe · fluent speech that does not make sense · poor understanding · often unaware of the problem
    • global later: PoM 2 Neurology: full MCA territory · no verbal output, understanding lost
    • conduction (arcuate fasciculus) extra: understands and can speak, but cannot repeat
  • Types of parietal lobe syndrome later: PoM 2 Neurology:
    • dominant (left) parietal: Gerstmann syndrome extra: right-left confusion, cannot write (agraphia), cannot calculate (acalculia), finger and object recognition problems
    • non-dominant (right) parietal: neglect: ignores one side of space (visual neglect) or of the body (sensory neglect) · does not recognize their own limb (asomatognosia) · denies the deficit (anosognosia) · trouble dressing (dressing apraxia)
    • apraxia: cannot carry out a movement on command (cannot pretend to use a hammer, form a hand posture, whistle or draw) despite normal power
    • cortical sensory loss (see section 6): primary sensation intact but stereognosis or graphesthesia lost
  • To say: "Alert and oriented, attentive, speech fluent at a normal rate and rhythm, no involuntary movements; mood good, affect appropriate."

Cranial nerves

PPES 2 · Weeks 8–9 · 17 manoeuvres

What to look for

Before the manoeuvres

  • Examine cranial nerves I to XII in an organized, logical order later: PoM 2 Neurology. The handbook's screening exam covers II, III, IV, VI, V, VII, IX, X and XII; I, VIII and XI are not part of the screening exam
  • The eye, ear, mouth and face tests are the same moves as the head and neck exam
  • General observation first, at rest: pupils (II, III) · eye position (III, IV, VI) · drooping lid (ptosis) · facial droop or asymmetry (VII) · articulation of words (VII, X, XII)

CN VIII, vestibulocochlear: hearing

Hearing

Picture
Steps
  1. Block one ear; whisper numbers or rub your fingers by the other, out of sight
  2. If abnormal: Weber (512 Hz fork on top of the head) and Rinne (fork on the mastoid, then beside the ear)

  3. Light into one eye: watch direct and consensual constriction

Pearls

Remember

  • The gag and corneal reflexes are in the course objectives but are not done on every patient; know how and why.
  • Upper vs lower motor neuron facial weakness: is the forehead spared? Spared = upper motor neuron (stroke); forehead weak too = lower motor neuron (Bell's palsy).
  • To say: "Cranial nerves II to XII are intact."

Motor

PPES 2 · Weeks 8–9 · 16 manoeuvres

What to look for

Before the manoeuvres

  • The motor exam has four parts: bulk, tone, power, reflexes (reflexes are in section 5)
  • Observation: scan for involuntary movements, muscle symmetry, atrophy (wasting) and fasciculations
    • fasciculations: small, irregular, spontaneous twitches of part of a muscle, a lower motor neuron sign; tapping or warming the muscle can bring them out
  • Muscle tone: the resistance you feel when you move a relaxed limb yourself
    • arms, patient sitting and relaxed: move the wrist (rigidity) · turn the forearm palm up, palm down (pronation-supination) quickly (spasticity) · move the elbow (both)
    • legs, patient lying and relaxed: roll the leg and bend it at the knee (rigidity) · lift the knee quickly with both hands (spasticity: the heel should stay on the bed) · with one hand bend the knee while the other pulls the foot up (spasticity)
    • normal, reduced (hypotonia, floppy), or increased (hypertonia: spastic or rigid); paratonia = the patient cannot relax during tone testing
    • spasticity vs rigidity:
      • spasticity (upper motor neuron): resistance depends on speed (worse the faster you move); clasp-knife: resistance builds, then suddenly gives way
      • rigidity (basal ganglia, the extrapyramidal system, for example Parkinson disease): resistance the same at any speed, from tightening of opposing muscles together; lead-pipe = smooth and even throughout · cogwheel = jerky, ratchet-like; it gets more obvious when the patient moves the other hand (augmentation)
  • Weakness is paresis; paralysis is plegia (hemiparesis, hemiplegia: one side)
  • Muscle power: test one muscle group at a time (only the deltoids, and hip abduction and adduction, can be tested both sides together); stabilize the joint you are testing and compare sides; against gravity, then against full resistance, graded out of 5
  • Describe what the patient can do, too: gets out of a chair without help, climbs stairs, walks on heels and toes, squats. Judge 5/5 for the person (a 94-year-old is not a 20-year-old boxer).
  • Power grading, Medical Research Council (MRC) scale, and name the muscle, nerve and root for each movement:
    • 0 no movement
    • 1 a flicker of contraction
    • 2 moves with gravity eliminated
    • 3 moves against gravity, no added resistance
    • 4 against some resistance, but not normal (4+ almost normal, 4, 4− more than against gravity but not much more); any 4 means the muscle is weak
    • 5 full power
  • The handbook's screening movements (starred) are shoulder abduction, elbow flexion and extension, hip flexion, knee extension and flexion, ankle dorsiflexion and plantar flexion, and big toe extension; its screening list adds wrist and finger extensors and the hand intrinsics

Pearls

Remember

  • Upper vs lower motor neuron lesion:
    • upper motor neuron (brain or spinal cord, e.g. stroke, multiple sclerosis): weakness in a pyramidal pattern (arm extensors: deltoids, triceps, wrist and finger extensors; leg flexors: hip flexion, knee flexion, ankle dorsiflexion, big toe extension), increased tone (spasticity), brisk reflexes, clonus (repeated rhythmic beats), upgoing plantar response, little wasting, no fasciculations
    • lower motor neuron (nerve root, nerve, neuromuscular junction or muscle): weakness matching the affected nerve or area, wasting, fasciculations, flaccid (low) tone, low or absent reflexes, toe down or no response
    • peripheral neuropathy: not pyramidal; distal flexors and extensors equally weak · myopathy: proximal weakness (shoulders, hips) · radiculopathy or single nerve: weak in that root's or nerve's area (e.g. S1 root, peroneal nerve)
    • just after a sudden upper motor neuron lesion (stroke, cord injury) the limb can be floppy at first, with spasticity developing over the following days to a week
  • To say: "Bulk and tone are normal; power is 5 out of 5 throughout."

Reflexes

PPES 2 · Weeks 8–9 · 7 manoeuvres

What to look for

Before the manoeuvres

  • The fourth part of the motor exam: the handbook does the reflexes and plantars right after power, before sensation
  • Deep tendon and plantar reflexes, and the nerve and root of each
  • Patient relaxed with the limb properly positioned; let the hammer swing
  • Note the grade and symmetry, and draw them on a stick figure. Grade each reflex (0–4):
    • 0 absent
    • 1+ present only with reinforcement
    • 2+ normal
    • 3+ brisk
    • 4+ clonus
  • Brisk reflexes point to an upper motor neuron lesion; reduced or absent to a lower motor neuron lesion
  • Reinforcement if a reflex will not come: arms: clench the teeth (or spell a word backwards) · legs: hook the hands together and pull them apart, then tap

  1. With the end of a broken tongue depressor, stroke the outer edge of the sole from the heel toward the toes

Sensory

PPES 2 · Weeks 8–9 · 9 manoeuvres

What to look for

Before the manoeuvres

  • The handbook's screening sensory exam is pinprick (small fibre) and vibration (large fibre); the other modalities are for when a problem is found
  • Two things to work out: the pattern (nerve, root, spinal cord or hemisphere) and the type of fibre (small: pain and temperature · large: vibration and position)
  • Name what the patient feels: paresthesia (pins and needles, tingling) or allodynia (light touch hurts)
  • Explain and demonstrate on a normal area first (for example the forehead), then test with the patient's eyes closed
  • Ask "Does that (left) feel about the same as this (right)?" side to side and distal to proximal. Asking "is there a difference?" invites minor differences. If one side is reduced, ask them to put a number on it ("if this side is 100% sharp, how sharp is that one?")
  • Diagram any sensory abnormality you find
  • Two pathways, two sets of modalities later: PoM 2 Neurology:
    • spinothalamic tract: pain and temperature (and crude touch); fibres cross within a level or two of entering the cord
    • dorsal (posterior) columns: vibration and joint position (proprioception) (and fine touch); fibres stay on the same side until the brainstem
  • Posterior column vs spinothalamic lesions later: PoM 2 Neurology:
    • posterior cord lesion: lost vibration and proprioception, pain and temperature kept (for example vitamin B12 deficiency, syphilis (tabes dorsalis)); positive Romberg (section 8)
    • anterior cord lesion: lost pain and temperature and power below the level, vibration and proprioception kept
    • central cord: pain and temperature lost in a band ("suspended"), hands weaker than shoulders
    • half the cord (Brown-Séquard): same side weakness and lost vibration and proprioception · opposite side lost pain and temperature
    • a sensory level (normal above a line, reduced below) points to the spinal cord
  • Rough distribution of the dermatomes C5–T1 and L2–S1: C5 lateral shoulder and arm · C6 thumb and index finger · C7 middle finger · C8 ring and little finger · T1 medial arm · T10 umbilicus · L2 front of the upper thigh · L3 front of the lower thigh and knee · L4 medial leg · L5 lateral leg and top of the foot · S1 lateral foot and sole
  • Test cortical sensation only once primary sensation is intact: a primary loss cannot be pinned to the cortex

Steps
  1. Eyes closed
  2. Draw a number on the palm with a blunt tip
  3. Ask them to name it
Positive

Cannot name it although primary sensation is intact → parietal (sensory cortex)

Extinction (both sides at once)

Parietal lobe syndrome: sensory neglect later: PoM 2 Neurology

Picture
Steps
  1. Eyes closed
  2. Touch one hand, then the other: "Which side?"
  3. Then touch both at once
  4. Visual version: wiggle fingers in both fields at once
Positive

Feels each side alone but misses one side when both are touched together → parietal (visual extinction → occipital) (normal: "both")

Two-point discrimination

Cortical sensation (fine touch) extra

Picture
Steps
  1. Eyes closed
  2. Touch the fingertip with one or two points of an opened paper clip
  3. Ask "one or two?"
  4. Narrow the gap until they cannot tell
Positive

Needs a wider gap than the other side (normal fingertip: about 2–5 mm)

Pearls

Remember

  • Primary sensory loss (pinprick, temperature, vibration) can come from anywhere along the pathway; only cortical sensory loss with intact primary sensation points to the cortex.
  • To say: "Primary sensation is intact to light touch, pinprick, temperature, vibration and joint position; stereognosis and graphesthesia are normal."

Coordination

PPES 2 · Weeks 8–9 · 3 manoeuvres

What to look for

Before the manoeuvres

  • Test cerebellar function: also listen to the speech (slurred, irregular) and watch the eye movements (nystagmus)
  • Cerebellar signs appear on the same side as the lesion
  • Arms sitting (finger-nose, then rapid alternating movements); heel-shin lying

Arms

Finger-nose

Cerebellar function: dysmetria and intention tremor

Picture
Steps
  1. Hold your finger far enough that their arm reaches at least 90% of full extension
  2. Patient touches their nose, then your finger, several times
  3. Move your finger between touches; watch the path and whether they hit the target
Positive

Overshoots or misses (dysmetria), tremor worse near the target (intention tremor) (normal: smooth and accurate)

Rapid alternating movements

Cerebellar function: dysdiadochokinesia

Picture
Steps
  1. Hands: one hand palm up in the other, also palm up
  2. Turn the top hand back and forth (back of the hand, then palm) as fast as they can
  3. Watch and listen for a regular rhythm; each side in turn
  4. Feet: tap the foot on the floor or your hand quickly
Positive

Slow, clumsy, irregular alternation (dysdiadochokinesia) (normal: fast, even rhythm)

Legs

Heel-shin

Cerebellar function in the legs

Picture
Steps
  1. Patient lying on the back
  2. Put the right heel on the left kneecap
  3. Draw a straight line down the shin with the heel
  4. Lift the heel high and bring it back to the kneecap; repeat several times
  5. Other side
Positive

Heel wobbles or falls off the shin (normal: smooth, stays on the shin)

Pearls

Remember

  • To say: "Finger-nose and heel-shin are smooth and accurate with no intention tremor; rapid alternating movements are normal in rate, rhythm and smoothness."

Balance and gait

PPES 2 · Weeks 8–9 · 2 manoeuvres

What to look for

Before the manoeuvres

  • Everything standing, last: the handbook does balance (Romberg) and then gait; tandem walking and Romberg can go together while the patient is up
  • Perform and interpret Romberg's test: what is it testing? Balance needs two of three inputs (joint position sense, the vestibular system, vision); closing the eyes takes vision away
  • Gait: walk across the room, turn around and come back: posture, balance, arm swing, stride, turning
  • Walk on the toes (ankle plantar flexion strength, gastrocnemius) and on the heels (ankle dorsiflexion strength, tibialis anterior): this is part of the motor exam
  • Tandem gait (heel to toe in a straight line): the most sensitive screen for ataxia (unsteady, poorly coordinated walking)
  • Squat and stand back up: proximal leg power (hips and thighs)
  • Cerebellar gait: wide-based, staggering, swaying while standing; falls toward the side of a one-sided lesion
  • Reduced arm swing, small quick steps that speed up (festination) and a stooped posture suggest Parkinson disease

Balance

Romberg test

Sensory ataxia (lost joint position sense) vs cerebellar ataxia

Picture
Steps
  1. Patient stands with feet together, eyes open, no support
  2. Stand with them, ready to catch
  3. Patient closes the eyes for 5–10 seconds
  4. Watch for swaying or stepping off
Positive

Steady with eyes open but becomes unstable and steps off with eyes closed = positive Romberg = sensory ataxia (dorsal columns or nerves: B12 deficiency, peripheral neuropathy) · unsteady even with eyes open = cerebellar ataxia (Romberg not positive) (normal: steady both ways)

Walking

Tandem gait

Ataxia

Picture
Steps
  1. Walk heel to toe in a straight line (the roadside sobriety test)
  2. Each heel touches the toe of the last step
  3. Stand close by
Positive

Cannot keep the line, steps off or sways (normal: steady)

Pearls

Remember

  • To say: "Romberg negative; gait normal; heel, toe and tandem walking steady; squats and stands without help."

Where is the lesion?

PoM 2 · Neurology block · 11 levels

What to look for

The approach

  • Explain and justify where the lesion is in the nervous system (the neuraxis) later: PoM 2 Neurology
  • Use the same three steps for every neurological problem:
    • describe the problem: onset (acute, subacute, chronic) · course (progressive, intermittent, stable) · deficits (cognitive, motor, sensory, coordination)
    • localize the lesion from your exam findings
    • offer a differential diagnosis from the description and the localization; localizing tells you which investigations to order
  • Ask four questions of your findings:
    • upper or lower motor neuron? (section 4) later: PoM 2 Neurology
    • what pattern of weakness? one side (pyramidal) · both legs or all four limbs · one root (myotomal) · one nerve · distal on both sides · proximal on both sides · eyes first
    • is sensation involved, and in what pattern? none · a sensory level · one dermatome · glove and stocking · crossed (face one side, body the other)
    • any cortical, brainstem or cerebellar signs? (sections 2, 3 and 7)
  • Work down the neuraxis: brain → brainstem → spinal cord → anterior horn cell → nerve root → plexus → nerve → neuromuscular junction → muscle
  • The tempo predicts the type of lesion: hyperacute = ischemic stroke or hemorrhage · acute = trauma · subacute = inflammation or infection · subacute to chronic = tumour

Brain

Cortex

Weakness on one side, face and arm more than leg (upper motor neuron)

Picture
Steps
  1. Upper motor neuron signs on one side
  2. Look for a cortical sign: aphasia (dominant side), neglect (non-dominant side), a homonymous hemianopia
  3. Check cortical sensation: stereognosis, graphesthesia, extinction
Positive

Any one cortical sign puts it in the cortex · e.g. left middle cerebral artery (MCA) stroke: right face and arm weaker than leg, plus expressive aphasia

Subcortex (white matter, internal capsule)

One side, face, arm and leg alike

Picture
Steps
  1. Upper motor neuron signs on one side
  2. No cortical signs
  3. Is it pure motor, pure sensory, or mild weakness with mild clumsiness on the same side?
Positive

Pure motor or pure sensory hemibody, or ataxic hemiparesis, with no cortical signs · internal capsule: motor and sensory together

Brainstem

Upper motor neuron weakness plus cranial nerve findings

Picture
Steps
  1. Upper motor neuron signs
  2. Vertigo, or double vision, plus another symptom
  3. Is sensation crossed (face on one side, body on the other)?
Positive

Crossed signs, or cranial nerve findings with long-tract signs

Cerebellum

Coordination, not power

Picture
Steps
  1. Listen to the speech: slurred?
  2. Watch the eyes: nystagmus?
  3. Finger-nose, rapid alternating movements, heel-shin
  4. Gait and tandem walking
Positive

Ataxia on the same side as the lesion, power normal · e.g. slurred speech plus right finger-nose ataxia → right cerebellum

Spinal cord

Spinal cord

Both legs (paraparesis) or all four limbs (quadriparesis), upper motor neuron

Picture
Steps
  1. Upper motor neuron signs below the lesion: brisk reflexes, upgoing plantars
  2. Find a sensory level
  3. Ask about bladder and bowel
  4. Ask about neck or back pain
Positive

Upper motor neuron signs in both legs (or all four limbs) plus a sensory level · cervical cord → all four limbs, thoracic → legs · e.g. cervical spondylosis

Anterior horn cell

Weakness that varies, upper and lower motor neuron signs together

Picture
Steps
  1. Wasting and fasciculations (lower motor neuron)
  2. Brisk reflexes in the same limbs (upper motor neuron)
  3. Test sensation
Positive

Both kinds of motor neuron signs, sensation normal · e.g. amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)

Peripheral (lower motor neuron)

Nerve root

One myotome, lower motor neuron

Picture
Steps
  1. Weakness only in that root's muscles
  2. That root's reflex reduced
  3. Pinprick in that dermatome
  4. Ask about neck or back pain shooting down the limb
Positive

Myotomal weakness, dermatomal sensory loss and the matching reflex reduced, with pain · e.g. disc herniation (radiculopathy)

Plexus

One limb, several nerves, lower motor neuron

Picture
Steps
  1. Weakness across more than one nerve and root in one limb
  2. Sensory loss in that limb
  3. Ask about pain
Positive

One limb, more than one nerve, usually painful · e.g. brachial neuritis

Peripheral nerve

One nerve's muscles, or distal more than proximal on both sides

Picture
Steps
  1. Weakness in one nerve's area, or feet before hands
  2. Sensory loss in that nerve, or glove and stocking
  3. Ankle reflexes
Positive

Single nerve, or length-dependent: feet first, glove-and-stocking loss, ankle jerks reduced or absent · e.g. Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS)

Neuromuscular junction

Eyes first, then speech and swallowing; fluctuates

Picture
Steps
  1. Droopy lids (ptosis), double vision
  2. Does weakness worsen with use?
  3. Test sensation and reflexes
Positive

Fatigable, descending weakness, eyes first, with normal sensation and reflexes · e.g. myasthenia gravis

Muscle

Proximal and symmetric: shoulder and hip girdle, neck, trunk

Picture
Steps
  1. Trouble rising from a chair, climbing stairs, lifting the arms
  2. Test sensation
  3. Test reflexes
Positive

Proximal symmetric weakness, no sensory loss, reflexes often normal · e.g. dermatomyositis

Pearls

Remember

  • Upper vs lower motor neuron: upper = no wasting, no fasciculations, brisk reflexes, spastic tone, upgoing plantar, pyramidal pattern · lower = wasting, fasciculations possible (anterior horn cell, nerve), reflexes normal, reduced or absent, toe down, a pattern that fits the level
  • Both legs weak can come from a parasagittal brain lesion (e.g. a meningioma), the spinal cord, anterior horn cells, several lumbosacral roots (e.g. spinal stenosis), a polyneuropathy or muscle
  • Slurred speech: dysarthria or aphasia? Test comprehension, naming, repetition and fluency (section 2). All normal = dysarthria, an articulation problem, which can come from the cortex, subcortex, thalamus, brainstem, cerebellum, a nerve, the neuromuscular junction or muscle; what comes with it localizes
  • Middle cerebral artery (MCA) stroke: face and arm worse than leg · frontal → mostly motor · parietal → mostly sensory · internal capsule → both
  • Worked example (lecture case 1): sudden right facial droop, right arm much weaker than the leg, no speech but follows commands, no neglect → expressive aphasia with right face and arm weakness → left frontal lobe, left MCA territory; hyperacute → stroke or hemorrhage
  • ⚠ New weakness with a sensory level, or bladder and bowel symptoms, points to the spinal cord and needs urgent assessment
  • To say: "Upper motor neuron signs in both legs with a sensory level at the umbilicus (T10) localize to the thoracic spinal cord."
