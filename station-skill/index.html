How a station runs

Every station runs the same way: read the door, knock and introduce yourself, do the task out loud, then close and report. Below is what each station marks, the opening line for each exam, and how to finish. Built from the course Clinical Skills objectives and the course objectives; no station scenario or marking sheet is reproduced.

General approach

Every station

The general approach

  • Say it first (TOSCE): "Hello, my name is ___, I'm a medical student. Could you tell me your full name and date of birth? Today I've been asked to ___. Is that okay with you? Please let me know at any point if anything is uncomfortable."
  • Read the door: the instructions posted outside the room tell you the task, the patient and what you must report or answer
    • read the whole thing, twice; then plan your first three steps before you knock
  • Enter and introduce: knock (students report this earns a mark) · introduce yourself and your role · confirm the patient's identity (full name and date of birth) · explain what you will do · ask for consent
  • Prepare: wash your hands and clean your stethoscope (yes, even in a history-only station) · warm your hands and instruments · check comfort: "Are you comfortable? How is the lighting and temperature in the room?"
    • position and drape for the exam you are about to do (section 4)
  • The task: work in a fixed order so nothing is missed and the patient is not sat up and laid down repeatedly
    • explain each step in plain words before you do it and watch the patient's face for pain
    • say your findings out loud as you go: "No signs of respiratory distress."
    • choose words with care: say "table" not "bed", and "examine", "press" or "tap" rather than "touch"
  • Close and report: thank the patient, help them sit up or cover up, wash your hands, then give the examiner a short summary of your findings and answer any follow-up questions
  • Leave the phone outside: phones and smart watches are not allowed in the stations

Reading the door

TOSCE and OSCE

How to open it

What to say

  • Integrate communication and clinical skills to demonstrate an approach to a clinical scenario, using clinical reasoning to formulate and prioritize a differential diagnosis and management plan, if required
  • A station asks for one of three things: a history, a physical exam, or a history and exam together; some add follow-up questions from the examiner at the end
  • What to pull from the door, in the first 30 seconds:
    • who: the patient's name and age, so you can greet them and confirm identity
    • what: the verb ("take a history", "examine", "perform a focused exam"); a focused exam means only the parts that answer the question, not a head-to-toe exam
    • which system: decides your position, draping and equipment before you go in
    • what to finish with: report findings, give a differential, or explain a plan to the patient
  • Time: each station is timed and a signal warns you near the end (student accounts describe stations of about 7 minutes with a warning at 5; check the instructions on the day)
    • at the warning, move to the steps that carry the most marks and leave time to close
  • If you finish early, it is fine to summarize back to the patient and ask whether there is anything else; do not invent extra steps

What is marked

TOSCE and OSCE · 6 topics

How to open it

What to say

  • The TOSCE assesses communication skills, physical exam technique, organization, professionalism and the ability to accept constructive feedback
  • The OSCE assesses communication, physical exam technique, organization and professionalism, along with taking a relevant history, a focused exam, giving a differential diagnosis, and clinical reasoning and judgement
  • Two kinds of mark per station:
    • a task-specific checklist of steps (most of the mark): did you do each step, and do it correctly?
    • a global rating: the examiner's overall judgement of your verbal and nonverbal communication, organization and logic, and patient-centred approach
  • TOSCE: a formative (practice) assessment; after each station you get feedback and coaching on what you just did
  • OSCE: covers the clinical skills sessions from both years; you are expected to perform without prompting
  • The assessment form for the primary physical exam skills rates you against the course's Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs, the tasks a graduate must be trusted to do) and competencies as meeting or not meeting expectations, with written comments

Talking with the patient

Communication

Rapport, plain language, listening, nonverbal cues (eye contact, open posture)

What to do
  1. Introduce yourself and your role, confirm identity
  2. Explain before each step, in words the patient knows
  3. Check in: "Is that okay?" "Any pain?"
  4. Pick up on cues (a wince, a worried look) and respond
Common slip

Talking to the examiner instead of the patient, or using jargon with the patient

Doing the exam

Physical exam technique

Correct steps, done properly, on the correct landmarks

What to do
  1. Follow the system's order (for most: look → feel → tap → listen)
  2. Compare left with right
  3. Use the instrument correctly (bell versus diaphragm, cuff size)
Common slip

Listening through the gown, or skipping a step and saying "I would" without doing it

Organization

A logical sequence with no doubling back

What to do
  1. Do everything you need with the patient sitting, then everything lying
  2. Finish one region before moving on
Common slip

Sitting the patient up and down repeatedly, or jumping between systems

Looking after the patient

Professionalism

Respect, privacy, consent, safety

What to do
  1. Wash hands and clean the stethoscope before and after
  2. Drape so only the area being examined is exposed
  3. Stop if the patient asks
Common slip

Forgetting hand hygiene or leaving the patient uncovered (draping is a marked point)

Thinking it through

Clinical reasoning (OSCE, some TOSCE stations)

Linking findings to a likely diagnosis

What to do
  1. Summarize the key positives and negatives
  2. Give the most likely diagnosis first, then the dangerous ones not to miss
  3. Suggest a next step if asked
Common slip

Listing every possible diagnosis with no order or reason

Accepting feedback (TOSCE)

Openness to coaching

What to do
  1. Listen to the feedback without arguing
  2. Say what you will do differently next time
Common slip

Defending or explaining away a missed step

Pearls

Remember

  • The examiner cannot read your mind: name each step and each finding out loud, or as student accounts put it, "vocalize what you are doing"
  • When time is short, it is acceptable to say what you would normally add ("I would normally also check orthostatic blood pressure, but I'm mindful of time"), but do the core steps

Opening lines for each exam

PPES · FoM and PoM 1 · 7 topics

How to open it

What to say

  • The common opener, from the course final paper: introduce yourself → confirm name and date of birth → check comfort (lighting, temperature) → ask them to sit on the table → sanitize your hands and stethoscope → explain what today's exam involves → ask for consent
  • Then adapt what you say you will do, how the patient sits or lies, and what you uncover to the exam
  • Ask for the vital signs at the start of a system exam: heart rate (HR), blood pressure (BP), respiratory rate (RR), temperature and oxygen saturation (as on the course respiratory and abdominal pages)

Chest and belly

Vital signs and general inspection (PPES Week 1)

A calm, comfortable patient, sitting correctly for blood pressure

What to do
  1. Say: "Today I'll take a general look at you and check your vital signs: your pulse, breathing rate and blood pressure."
  2. Seat them with legs uncrossed, feet on the floor, arm resting on a pillow at heart level
  3. Ask: "Would you like me to take your temperature?"
Common slip

Not offering the other arm ("Would you like me to do it on the other side?") or forgetting to mention orthostatic BP

Respiratory (PPES Week 2)

Back exposed for the posterior chest, then front, with draping

What to do
  1. Say: "I've been asked to examine your breathing. I'll look at, feel, tap on and listen to your chest, back first, then front."
  2. Patient sits on the edge of the table, gown open at the back
  3. Ask for the vital signs
Common slip

Not draping the front of the chest while examining the back

Tap to reveal

Abdomen (PPES Week 3)

Patient lying flat and relaxed, abdomen exposed

What to do
  1. Say: "I'll be examining your abdomen: looking, listening, tapping and pressing gently. Is there any pain? I'll start away from that spot."
  2. Patient lies flat (supine), one pillow, arms by the sides
  3. Expose from just below the chest to the hip bones, drape the rest
Common slip

Pressing on the painful area first, or not watching the face while palpating

Tap to reveal

Cardiovascular (PPES Week 4)

Head of the table raised, standing on the patient's right

What to do
  1. Say: "I'll be examining your heart and circulation: looking at your neck, feeling your pulses and listening to your chest."
  2. Raise the head of the table to about 30-45° (the angle for seeing the jugular venous pressure, JVP)
  3. Stand on the patient's right
Common slip

Patient lying flat or sitting bolt upright, so the neck veins cannot be seen

Head, nerves and joints

Head and neck (PPES Week 7)

Patient sitting at your eye level, equipment ready

What to do
  1. Say: "I'll be examining your head and neck: your eyes, ears, nose, mouth and throat, and feeling your neck and thyroid."
  2. Patient sits, your eyes level with theirs
  3. Have the penlight, otoscope, ophthalmoscope and a cup of water (for the thyroid swallow) ready
Common slip

Fetching equipment mid-exam, or not cleaning the otoscope tip

Tap to reveal

Neurological (PPES Weeks 8 and 9)

Patient sitting on the edge of the table, able to stand for gait

What to do
  1. Say: "I'll be checking your nerves: how your eyes and face move, your strength, feeling, reflexes and balance. Some parts mean following my instructions, and I'll show you each one."
  2. Patient sits on the edge of the table, legs hanging free for reflexes
  3. Reflex hammer and tuning fork ready
Common slip

Not demonstrating an instruction, or not comparing left with right

Tap to reveal

Musculoskeletal (PPES Week 10)

Screening questions first, both sides exposed

What to do
  1. Ask the three GALS (Gait, Arms, Legs, Spine) screening questions: "Any pain or stiffness in your muscles, joints or back? Can you dress yourself without difficulty? Can you walk up and down stairs without difficulty?"
  2. Say: "Let me know if anything hurts and I'll stop."
  3. Examine standing, then sitting, then lying, with both sides uncovered to compare
Common slip

Moving a painful joint without asking about pain first

History stations

Interviewing · FoM to T2C

How to open it

What to say

  • Say it first (TOSCE): "Hello, my name is ___, I'm a medical student working with Dr. ___. I'd like to hear about what's been going on. Is it okay if I ask you some questions today? I'll take a few notes as we talk."
  • Still wash your hands at the start, even though you will not examine (students flag this as a marked step)
  • Open with an open question: "What brings you in today? Is there anything else you'd like to make sure we cover?"
  • History of presenting illness (HPI): site, onset, character, radiation, associated symptoms, timeline, what makes it worse or better, what they have tried, severity
  • The patient's perspective (FIFE): Feelings about it · Ideas about the cause · effect on Function (work, sleep, relationships) · Expectations for today
  • Signpost each change of topic: "I'd now like to ask about your past health."
  • Then past medical history, medications (dose and frequency, including over-the-counter and herbal), allergies (with the reaction), habits, social history, family history and a review of the relevant systems
  • Close: "Thank you for helping me understand what you've been going through. Before we finish, is there anything else important for me to know?" Summarize the key concerns back to the patient

Closing and reporting

TOSCE and OSCE · 3 topics

How to open it

What to say

  • With the patient: thank them, help them sit up or dress, offer the gown back, wash your hands
  • To the examiner: a short, ordered summary in the same sequence you examined
    • general inspection first: "Ms. ___ is a ___-year-old woman who looks well, is well-groomed and oriented, calm, in no distress."
    • then the numbers: "Heart rate ___, regular; respiratory rate ___; blood pressure ___ in both arms; temperature ___."
    • then the system findings, positives and the important negatives: "No cyanosis, pallor, jaundice or abnormal odours."
  • Follow-up questions: answer the question asked, briefly; if a differential is asked for, give the most likely first, then the dangerous ones you must not miss

Ending well

The warning signal

Priorities under time pressure

What to do
  1. Finish the step you are on
  2. Jump to the highest-yield remaining steps (for an exam, usually listening)
  3. Leave time to thank the patient

Running out of time mid-exam with no closing at all

Professionalism to the end

What to do

  2. Replace the drape or gown
  3. Wash your hands

The summary

Organized, accurate reporting

What to do
  1. General inspection
  2. Vital signs
  3. System findings in exam order, with key negatives

Reporting findings you did not elicit (examiners know what the standardized patient shows)

Pearls

Remember

  • ⚠ Never fake a finding: if you could not hear or feel something, say so ("I could not feel the apex beat; I would try the left lateral position")
  • A standardized patient may have no abnormal findings; a careful normal report earns the marks

TOSCE versus OSCE

FoM to T2C · 4 topics

How to open it

What to say

  • TOSCEs run after each block of physical exam teaching in Year 1 (after PPES Part 1 and Part 2, plus an end-of-year TOSCE) and twice in Year 2
    • each tests the skills taught in the sessions just before it
    • feedback is given straight after each station, so treat it as coaching
  • The OSCE comes near the end of Year 2, before the Consolidation of Skills block, and covers content from both years: histories, focused exams, differentials and plans
  • Same habits for both: the same opener, the same order, the same close; practise them until they are automatic so your attention goes to the patient and the findings
