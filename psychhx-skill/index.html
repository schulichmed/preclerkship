General approach

Every psychiatric interview

The general approach

  • Say it first (TOSCE, Teaching Objective Structured Clinical Exam): "Some of the questions I'll ask are personal. I ask them of everyone I see, and you can tell me if you'd rather not answer something."
  • Two components, held in balance
    • Content: information and symptoms, which build the diagnostic hypothesis
    • Process: rapport and empathy, the relationship that lets the patient trust you with sensitive information
  • Interview skills: use greeting, open-ended questions, effective listening, empathic comments, paraphrasing, summarizing and reassurance
    • funnel strategy: start open ("What brought you to the hospital today?"), then specific ("How is your anxiety interfering with your life?"), then closed, verifying questions ("Do you isolate yourself when depressed?")
    • empathic comment: "That sounds frightening." · paraphrase: "So the voices started after you stopped the medication?" · summarize at each change of section
  • Therapeutic alliance: build rapport while keeping appropriate boundaries (friendly, not a friend)
  • Transference and countertransference: recognize them and the effect they have on the patient-doctor relationship
    • transference: the patient unconsciously redirects feelings about someone from their past onto you (e.g. treats you like a dismissive parent)
    • countertransference: your own unconscious reaction to the patient (e.g. dreading a patient, or giving one who reminds you of a friend extra time)
  • Your own biases: recognize personal biases and keep them from interfering with the patient's best interests; afterwards, reflect on the interview for bias and transference/countertransference
  • Adjust to the situation: a focused, structured interview: adjust the history and MSE to the patient's complaint and the setting (an emergency needs safety first; a clinic follow-up needs the course of illness)

The psychiatric screen

PoM 2 · Psychiatry · 9 areas to ask about

How to open it

What to say

  • Screen every major group, even when the complaint points to one: a depressed patient may also be manic, anxious or drinking. This is how you recognize and screen for the major disorders: anxiety, mood, substance use, personality, psychotic and cognitive.

Mood and anxiety

Depression

A major depressive episode

Ask or assess
  1. "Have you been feeling down or depressed, or lost interest in things, most of the day, nearly every day, for 2 weeks or more?"
Listen for

Low mood or loss of interest for 2 weeks or more · then SIGECAPS: Sleep, Interest, Guilt, Energy, Concentration, Appetite, Psychomotor change, Suicide

Tap to reveal

Mania or hypomania

Bipolar disorder

Ask or assess
  1. "Have you ever had a time when you felt so 'up' or full of energy that you got into trouble, or others said you weren't yourself?"
Listen for

Elevated or irritable mood (not while intoxicated) · less need for sleep · fast, pressured speech · racing thoughts · grandiosity · risky behaviour · ⚠ always ask about mania in anyone depressed · DIGFAST (3 or more, 4 if only irritable): Distractibility · Indiscretion (spending, sex) · Grandiosity · Flight of ideas · Activity up or agitation · Sleep need down · Talkative (pressured)

Tap to reveal

Anxiety

Generalized anxiety, panic, phobias, trauma

Ask or assess
  1. "Do you worry a lot about everyday things? Have you had sudden rushes of fear with a racing heart?"
Listen for

Hard-to-control worry · panic attacks peaking within minutes · avoidance · nightmares or flashbacks after a trauma · generalized anxiety: worry most days for 6 months or more, plus 3 of 6: restless (keyed up) · easily tired · poor concentration (mind going blank) · irritable · muscle tension · poor sleep (BE SKIM)

Trauma

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)

Ask or assess
  1. "Has something very frightening or upsetting happened to you? Do you have frightening dreams about it, or flashbacks where it feels like it's happening again? Do you avoid places or thoughts that remind you of it?"
Listen for

Re-experiencing (nightmares, flashbacks) · avoidance · numbing, can't enjoy things · on edge · a month or more (TRAUMA: Traumatic event · Re-experiencing · Avoidance · Unable to function · Month or more · Arousal)

Pearls

Remember

  • Tentative diagnosis: apply the DSM-5 (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th edition) criteria to make a tentative diagnosis of anxiety, depression, psychosis, substance use, personality and cognitive disorders. The screen tells you which criteria set to check.
  • Normal sadness (e.g. bereavement) vs a mood disorder: grief comes in waves tied to reminders of the loss, with self-esteem preserved; a depressive episode is pervasive, with worthlessness, guilt and loss of function.
  • Situational stress vs an anxiety disorder vs drugs or a medical condition: is the worry in proportion to a real stressor, and does it settle? Ask about caffeine, stimulants, alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal, and medical causes (e.g. thyroid).
  • Primary psychotic disorder vs other causes of psychosis: think substance intoxication or withdrawal and medical conditions (e.g. delirium) when onset is sudden, the patient is older, or hallucinations are visual.
  • Long-enduring patterns vs short-lived episodes: a personality disorder is a pattern present since adolescence or early adulthood, across situations; mood and psychotic disorders come in episodes with a "last well" date.

Safety: suicide and homicide

PoM 2 · Psychiatry · 9 areas to ask about

How to open it

What to say

  • Ask directly and calmly. Be honest about why you ask, take the time to listen, validate the person's story and their decision to seek help, and stay non-judgmental.
  • If an answer doesn't fit the question, reframe the question: patients can misunderstand, or avoid.
  • Self-injury is not the same as suicidal ideation: explore what it does for the person. It is easily missed while looking for the bigger safety concerns.

Pearls

Remember

  • ⚠ Red flags: active threats · talking about wanting to die · seeking access to means · hopelessness · unexpected talk or writing about death.
  • Risk is weighed from risk factors + warning signs + protective factors, and it is "not psychological math". In a severe-risk situation, protective factors may count for little.
  • ⚠ Decide whether the patient needs emergency care because of withdrawal symptoms, complications, violence, suicide or abuse, and act: stay with the patient, tell your supervisor, keep the setting safe.
    • inpatient vs outpatient depends on the acuity and severity of the risk and the safety net at home
    • in Ontario, any physician can complete a Form 1 (Application for Psychiatric Assessment) for an involuntary assessment when a mental disorder makes the person a danger to self or others
    • safety (crisis) plan: steps for a crisis · coping plan: strategies to stop stress escalating · ⚠ a "no-suicide contract" is not acceptable management
  • Collateral (family, family doctor) can confirm or challenge what you heard; privacy can be breached only when safety requires it, with care.

Past psychiatric history

PoM 2 · Psychiatry

How to open it

What to say

  • Goals: the longitudinal course of the illness · its severity · the predisposing, precipitating and protective factors
  • The illness: age of onset · first symptoms and triggers · functioning before and after · number and length of hospitalizations
  • Impact: "How is your life different because of the illness?" · "How do you cope?" · "What opportunities were lost?"
  • Treatments
    • biological: medications (names, doses, how long) · electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) · repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) · bright light therapy (BLT)
    • psychosocial: which psychotherapy, and what strategies were learned
    • "Has anything made a difference?"
  • Add to the MSE: insight (does the patient appreciate their symptoms and what drives them?) and judgement (decisions that led to action: leaving an abusive relationship, starting therapy, getting sober)

Pearls

Remember

  • Mood is the climate, affect is the weather. Report mood in the patient's own words.
  • Hallucinations are not always illness: on falling asleep or waking (hypnagogic, hypnopompic), or with poor vision (Charles Bonnet syndrome).
  • Phenomenology: describe how the patient experiences a symptom, not just its name. "Auditory hallucinations" doesn't tell you what the voices are like: ask the volume, whose voice, what they say, whether the patient talks back.

Cognitive screen (MoCA)

PoM 2 · Psychiatry · 8 areas to ask about

How to open it

What to say

  • Demonstrate skill in using the MoCA: about 10 minutes, out of 30; below 26 suggests cognitive impairment. It catches mild cognitive impairment better than the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE).
  • Read the instructions exactly as written and don't give hints. Make sure glasses and hearing aids are on.

Winding down and plan

PoM 2 · Psychiatry

How to open it

What to say

  • Winding down: don't finish on a sensitive topic · thank the patient · acknowledge their commitment · ask if anything was missed · ask FIFE: the patient's Feelings, Ideas, Function and Expectations about the problem · ask how they feel now · discuss the plan
  • Formulation: the biological, psychological and social factors that predisposed the patient, and those that protected them
  • Plan: a biopsychosocial initial treatment plan adapted to the patient
    • most urgent next steps · how to deal with safety issues · inpatient or outpatient
    • biological (e.g. medication) · psychological supports and therapy · social (housing, work, supports)
  • Explain it: communicate the working diagnosis and plan in clear, plain language the patient understands; find common ground when you disagree
  • Refer: help patients access the system for physical, psychological, social and economic rehabilitation or long-term care (e.g. community mental health, addiction services, social work)
  • Limits: seek help or consultation when needed, and accept you can't be "all things to all people": defer some requests, kindly.
  • Present the case: summarize the clinical, biological and psychosocial information to the group leader and peers, clearly and concisely: identification, chief complaint, HPI, pertinent screen positives and negatives, past history, MSE, risk assessment, tentative diagnosis, plan.
