The geriatric assessment

A comprehensive geriatric assessment looks at the whole older person, not one complaint: the history with a caregiver's view added, daily function, the extra review-of-systems questions, thinking and mood, and gait. Each part is below in the order you do it, with what to ask and what to listen for. No course notes cover this yet; built from the Clinical Skills objectives and student notes. Anything beyond the objectives is tagged extra, and where an objective has no source behind it (the older-adult gait method, the GDS depression scale, the problem list), the page says so.

General approach

Every geriatric assessment

The general approach

  • Say it first (TOSCE, Teaching Objective Structured Clinical Exam): "Hello, my name is [name], I'm a medical student working with Dr. [X]. I'd like to hear how things have been for you. Is it okay if I ask you some questions, and if [caregiver] adds what they have noticed?"
  • Perform a comprehensive geriatric assessment using a patient-centred approach, then develop a preliminary problem list and management plan (section 7)
  • Collateral history: a history from someone else who knows the patient (a spouse, child or other caregiver). Obtain a history from the older adult and their caregiver. Ask the patient's permission first, and let the patient speak before the caregiver adds
  • Make it easy for the patient: speak clearly and slowly, check they can hear and see you, and allow time
  • Always consider psychiatric causes of change: delirium, depression and MCI or dementia
  • Present the case to peers and faculty in a clear, concise way using the framework you are given, reflect on the encounter using the group's feedback, and give peers constructive feedback, communicating and cooperating with them respectfully

Safety and medicines

Falls and near-falls

Risk of injury

Ask or do
  1. "Have you fallen? Any near-falls?"
  2. Ask what happened just before
  3. Ask about medicines and poor eyesight
Listen or look for

Falls, near-falls, dizziness before, medicines or poor vision as causes

Medications and polypharmacy

Too many or wrongly taken medicines

Ask or do
  1. "Please tell me everything you take, including over-the-counter and herbal."
  2. Do you use a pill organizer (dosette)?
  3. Who sets them up? Do you ever miss or double a dose?
Listen or look for

Polypharmacy (many medicines) · missed or doubled doses

Wishes for care

Who decides if they cannot extra

Ask or do

Self-care

ADLs (basic self-care)

Whether they can look after themselves

Ask or do
  1. "Do you need any help with dressing, eating, getting around, using the toilet or washing?"
  2. Go through each one in turn
  3. Ask the caregiver too
Listen or look for

Dressing, Eating, Ambulating (walking), Toileting, Hygiene (bathing): DEATH

IADLs (living independently)

Whether they can run their own life

Ask or do
  1. "Who does your shopping, housework, bills and cooking?"
  2. Ask how they get around and use the phone
  3. Ask about managing their medicines
Listen or look for

Shopping, Housekeeping, Accounting (money), Food preparation, Transportation and telephone: SHAFT

Thinking and mood

CS2 · Geriatrics · 2 areas to ask about

The screening tests

  • Assess cognition and mood, say why the assessment matters, and interpret the results. The objectives name two screening tests: the MoCA (Montreal Cognitive Assessment, for thinking) and the GDS (Geriatric Depression Scale, for mood)
  • The MoCA has its own cards on the Psychiatric interview page. No source describes the GDS, so it is not on this page: check it against your session
  • ⚠ Don't use the MoCA in delirium (acute, fluctuating confusion)

Thinking and mood

Memory and confusion

Changes in thinking

Ask or do
  1. "Have you noticed any trouble with your memory?"
  2. Ask the caregiver what they have noticed
  3. Ask when it began and whether it came on suddenly
Listen or look for

Gradual decline (dementia) vs sudden change (delirium) · missed appointments, lost items, trouble with money

Mood

Depression

  3. Ask about thoughts of harming themselves

Low mood, lost interest · ⚠ thoughts of self-harm

Walking

Gait

How they walk and balance

Ask or do
  1. Watch them walk: posture, balance, arm swing, stride, turning
  2. Ask them to walk heel to toe along a straight line (tandem gait)
  3. Stand close by
Listen or look for

Cannot keep the line, steps off or sways · reduced arm swing, small steps and a stooped posture suggest Parkinson disease

Pearls

Remember

  • ⚠ Ask about falls whenever gait is abnormal: see the history (section 2).
